Theorem 5.3.3 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, Part 1.

If \(f(x)\) is continuous over an interval \([a,b],\) and the function \(A(x)\) is defined by
🔗
\begin{equation*} A(x)=\displaystyle{ \int_{a}^{x} f(t)\, dt}, \end{equation*}
then \(A'(x)=f(x)\) on \([a,b].\)
🔗
🔗

Proof.
Let \(x\) be a fixed interior point in the interval \([a,b]\text{.}\) We begin by studying difference quotients for the area function \(A\text{:}\)
🔗
\begin{align*} \frac{A(x+h)-A(x)}{h} \amp = \frac{1}{h}\left[\displaystyle{ \int_{a}^{x+h} f(t)\, dt}-\displaystyle{ \int_{a}^{x} f(t)\, dt}\right]\\ \amp = \frac{1}{h}\left[\displaystyle{ \int_{a}^{x+h} f(t)\, dt}+\displaystyle{ \int_{x}^{a} f(t) \,dt}\right]\\ \amp = \frac{1}{h}\displaystyle{ \int_{x}^{x+h} f(t)\, dt}. \end{align*}
For now, we assume \(h\gt 0\text{.}\) The integral \(\displaystyle \int_{x}^{x+h} f(t)\, dt \) describes the signed net area under the graph of \(f\) on the interval \([x, x+h]\text{.}\) Because \(f\) is continuous, the Extreme Value Theorem gives points \(x_m\) and \(x_M\) in the interval \([x, x+h]\) such that \(f(x_m)=m\) and \(f(x_M)=M\) are the absolute minimum and absolute maximum values of \(f\) on \([x, x+h]\text{,}\) respectively. Consequently,
🔗
\begin{equation*} m \leq f(t) \leq M \end{equation*}
for all \(t\) in \([x, x+h]\text{.}\) By properties of the definite integral, this gives
🔗
\begin{equation*} mh \leq \int_x^{x+h} f(t)\, dt \leq Mh \end{equation*}
Therefore,
🔗
\begin{equation*} m \leq \frac{1}{h}\int_x^{x+h} f(t)\, dt \leq M \end{equation*}
Or,
🔗
\begin{equation*} f(x_m) \leq \frac{1}{h}\int_x^{x+h} f(t)\, dt \leq f(x_M) \end{equation*}
This same inequality can be obtained when \(h \lt 0\) by applying the same argument to the interval \([x+h, x]\) (but carefully note how multiplying through by the negative number \(\frac{1}{h}\) influences inequalities).
🔗
Now as we allow \(h \rightarrow 0\text{,}\) the endpoint \(x+h\) approaches \(x\text{.}\) Because both \(x_m\) and \(x_M\) in the interval are between \(x\) and \(x+h\text{,}\) the continuity of \(f\) gives
🔗
\begin{equation*} \lim_{h \rightarrow 0} f(x_m)= \lim_{x_m \rightarrow x} f(x_m)=f(x) \end{equation*}
and
🔗
\begin{equation*} \lim_{h \rightarrow 0} f(x_M)= \lim_{x_M \rightarrow x} f(x_m)=f(x) \end{equation*}
Thus, applying the Squeeze Theorem to
🔗
\begin{equation*} f(x_m) \leq \frac{1}{h}\int_x^{x+h} f(t)\, dt \leq f(x_M) \end{equation*}
as \(h \rightarrow 0 \text{,}\) we obsere
🔗
\begin{equation*} \lim_{h \rightarrow 0} \frac{1}{h}\int_x^{x+h} f(t)\, dt =f(x) \end{equation*}
Note that if \(x=a\) or \(x=b\text{,}\) we simply apply the argument above using a one-sided limit. Returning to
🔗
\begin{equation*} \frac{A(x+h)-A(x)}{h}= \frac{1}{h}\int_x^{x+h} f(t)\, dt \end{equation*}
We obtain the desired
🔗
\begin{equation*} \lim_{h \rightarrow 0}\frac{A(x+h)-A(x)}{h}=f(x) \end{equation*}
or \(A'(x)=f(x).\)
