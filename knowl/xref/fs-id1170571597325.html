Example 5.3.16 A Roller-Skating Race.

James and Kathy are racing on roller skates. They race along a long, straight track, and whoever has gone the farthest after 5 sec wins a prize. If James can skate at a velocity of \(f(t)=5+2t\) ft/sec and Kathy can skate at a velocity of \(g(t)=10+\cos (\frac{\pi}{2}t)\) ft/sec, who is going to win the race?
🔗
Solution.
We need to integrate both functions over the interval \([0,5]\) and see which value is bigger. For James, we want to calculate
🔗
\begin{equation*} \displaystyle{ \int_{0}^{5} 5+2t \, dt}. \end{equation*}
Using the power rule, we have
🔗
\begin{align*} \displaystyle{ \int_{0}^{5} 5+2t \, dt}\amp =5t+t^2\biggr\vert_0^5\\ \amp =(25+25)-(0+0)=50. \end{align*}
Thus, James has skated 50 ft after 5 sec. Turning now to Kathy, we want to calculate
🔗
\begin{equation*} \displaystyle{ \int_{0}^{5} 10}+\cos \left(\dfrac{\pi}{2}t\right)\, dt. \end{equation*}
We know \(\sin t\) is an antiderivative of \(\cos t,\) so it is reasonable to expect that an antiderivative of \(\cos \left(\dfrac{\pi}{2}t\right)\) would involve \(\sin\left(\dfrac{\pi}{2}t\right).\) However, when we differentiate \(\sin\left(\dfrac{\pi}{2}t\right),\) we get \(\dfrac{\pi}{2}\cos\left(\dfrac{\pi}{2}t\right)\) as a result of the chain rule, so we have to account for this additional coefficient when we construct the antiderivative to integrate. We obtain
🔗
\begin{align*} \int_{0}^{5} 10+\cos \left(\dfrac{\pi}{2}t\right)\, dt\amp =10t+\dfrac{2}{\pi}\sin\left(\dfrac{\pi}{2}t\right)\biggr\vert_0^5\\ \amp =\left(50+\dfrac{2}{\pi}\right)-\left(0-\dfrac{2}{\pi}\sin 0\right)\\ \amp \approx 50.6. \end{align*}
Kathy has skated approximately 50.6 ft after 5 sec. Kathy wins, but not by much!
