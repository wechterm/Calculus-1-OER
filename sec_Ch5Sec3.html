    In the previous two sections, we looked at the definite integral and its relationship to the area under the curve of a function. Unfortunately, so far, the only tools we have available to calculate the value of a definite integral are geometric area formulas and limits of Riemann sums, and both approaches can be extremely cumbersome. In this section we look at some more powerful and useful techniques for evaluating definite integrals.
    🔗
    These new techniques rely on the relationship between differentiation and integration. This relationship was discovered and explored by both Sir Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (among others) during the late 1600s and early 1700s, and it is codified in what we now call the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, which has two parts that we examine in this section. Its very name indicates how central this theorem is to the entire development of calculus.
    🔗

    Note 5.3.1.

    Isaac Newton’s contributions to mathematics and physics changed the way we look at the world. The relationships he discovered, codified as Newton’s laws and the law of universal gravitation, are still taught as foundational material in physics today, and his calculus has spawned entire fields of mathematics. To learn more, read a brief biography of Newton with multimedia clips.

    Subsection 5.3.1 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus Part 1: Integrals and Antiderivatives

    Becuase the definite integral is a number, we can use the definite integral as a rule to define new functions. The first part of the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus discusses functions defined in terms of a definite integral.
    🔗

    Definition 5.3.2.

    Let \(f(x)\) be continuous over the interval \([a,b].\) The area function of \(f(x)\) on \([a,b]\) is given by
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\, dt \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Note that we have defined a function, \(A(x),\) as the definite integral of another function, \(f(t),\) from the point \(a\) to the point \(x\text{.}\) The key here is to notice that for any particular value of \(x\text{,}\) the definite integral is a number representing the signed net area under the graph of \(f\text{,}\) so the function \(A(x)\) outputs the signed net area under the graph of \(f\) on the interval \([a, x].\)
    🔗
    As mentioned earlier, the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus is an extremely powerful theorem that establishes the relationship between differentiation and integration, and gives us a way to evaluate definite integrals without using Riemann sums or calculating areas. The theorem is comprised of two parts, the first of which, the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, Part 1, is stated here. Part 1 establishes the relationship between differentiation and integration.
    🔗

    Theorem 5.3.3 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, Part 1.

    If \(f(x)\) is continuous over an interval \([a,b],\) and the function \(A(x)\) is defined by
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A(x)=\displaystyle{ \int_{a}^{x} f(t)\, dt}, \end{equation*}
    then \(A'(x)=f(x)\) on \([a,b].\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Let \(x\) be a fixed interior point in the interval \([a,b]\text{.}\) We begin by studying difference quotients for the area function \(A\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \frac{A(x+h)-A(x)}{h} \amp = \frac{1}{h}\left[\displaystyle{ \int_{a}^{x+h} f(t)\, dt}-\displaystyle{ \int_{a}^{x} f(t)\, dt}\right]\\ \amp = \frac{1}{h}\left[\displaystyle{ \int_{a}^{x+h} f(t)\, dt}+\displaystyle{ \int_{x}^{a} f(t) \,dt}\right]\\ \amp = \frac{1}{h}\displaystyle{ \int_{x}^{x+h} f(t)\, dt}. \end{align*}
    For now, we assume \(h\gt 0\text{.}\) The integral \(\displaystyle \int_{x}^{x+h} f(t)\, dt \) describes the signed net area under the graph of \(f\) on the interval \([x, x+h]\text{.}\) Because \(f\) is continuous, the Extreme Value Theorem gives points \(x_m\) and \(x_M\) in the interval \([x, x+h]\) such that \(f(x_m)=m\) and \(f(x_M)=M\) are the absolute minimum and absolute maximum values of \(f\) on \([x, x+h]\text{,}\) respectively. Consequently,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} m \leq f(t) \leq M \end{equation*}
    for all \(t\) in \([x, x+h]\text{.}\) By properties of the definite integral, this gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} mh \leq \int_x^{x+h} f(t)\, dt \leq Mh \end{equation*}
    Therefore,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} m \leq \frac{1}{h}\int_x^{x+h} f(t)\, dt \leq M \end{equation*}
    Or,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} f(x_m) \leq \frac{1}{h}\int_x^{x+h} f(t)\, dt \leq f(x_M) \end{equation*}
    This same inequality can be obtained when \(h \lt 0\) by applying the same argument to the interval \([x+h, x]\) (but carefully note how multiplying through by the negative number \(\frac{1}{h}\) influences inequalities).
    🔗
    Now as we allow \(h \rightarrow 0\text{,}\) the endpoint \(x+h\) approaches \(x\text{.}\) Because both \(x_m\) and \(x_M\) in the interval are between \(x\) and \(x+h\text{,}\) the continuity of \(f\) gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{h \rightarrow 0} f(x_m)= \lim_{x_m \rightarrow x} f(x_m)=f(x) \end{equation*}
    and
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{h \rightarrow 0} f(x_M)= \lim_{x_M \rightarrow x} f(x_m)=f(x) \end{equation*}
    Thus, applying the Squeeze Theorem to
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} f(x_m) \leq \frac{1}{h}\int_x^{x+h} f(t)\, dt \leq f(x_M) \end{equation*}
    as \(h \rightarrow 0 \text{,}\) we obsere
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{h \rightarrow 0} \frac{1}{h}\int_x^{x+h} f(t)\, dt =f(x) \end{equation*}
    Note that if \(x=a\) or \(x=b\text{,}\) we simply apply the argument above using a one-sided limit. Returning to
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{A(x+h)-A(x)}{h}= \frac{1}{h}\int_x^{x+h} f(t)\, dt \end{equation*}
    We obtain the desired
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{h \rightarrow 0}\frac{A(x+h)-A(x)}{h}=f(x) \end{equation*}
    or \(A'(x)=f(x).\)
    🔗
    🔗
    It is worth commenting on some of the key implications of this theorem. There is a reason it is called the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus. Not only does it establish a relationship between integration and differentiation, but also it guarantees that any integrable function on an interval \([a, b]\) has an antiderivative on \([a, b]\text{.}\) Specifically, it guarantees that any continuous function on \([a, b]\) has an antiderivative on \([a, b]\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Example 5.3.4 Finding a Derivative with the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.

    🔗
    \begin{equation*} g(x)=\displaystyle{ \int_{1}^{x} \frac{1}{t^3+1}\, dt.} \end{equation*}
    Solution.
    According to the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, the derivative is given by
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} g'(x) =\frac{1}{x^3+1}. \end{equation*}

    Example 5.3.6 Using the Fundamental Theorem and the Chain Rule to Calculate Derivatives.

    Let \(F(x)=\displaystyle{ \int_{1}^{\sqrt{x}} \sin t \, dt}.\) Find \(F'(x) .\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Letting \(u(x)=\sqrt{x},\) we have \(F(x)=\displaystyle{ \int_{1}^{u(x)} \sin tdt}.\) Thus, by the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus and the chain rule,
    🔗
    \begin{align*} F'(x) \amp =\sin(u(x))\frac{du}{dx} \\ \amp =\sin(u(x))\cdot (\frac{1}{2}x^{-1/2})\\ \amp =\frac{\sin\sqrt{x}}{2\sqrt{x}}. \end{align*}
    🔗

    Example 5.3.8 Using the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus with Two Variable Limits of Integration.

    Let \(F(x)=\displaystyle{ \int_{x}^{2x} t^3\, dt}.\) Find \(F'(x) .\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We have \(F(x)=\displaystyle{ \int_{x}^{2x} t^3dt}.\) Both limits of integration are variable, so we need to split this into two integrals. We get
    🔗
    \begin{align*} F(x)\amp =\displaystyle{ \int_{x}^{2x} t^3\, dt}\\ \amp =\displaystyle{ \int_{x}^{0} t^3\, dt}+\displaystyle{ \int_{0}^{2x} t^3\, dt}\\ \amp =- \displaystyle{ \int_{0}^{x} t^3\, dt}+\displaystyle{ \int_{0}^{2x} t^3\, dt}. \end{align*}
    Differentiating the first term, we obtain
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d}{dx}\left[- \displaystyle{ \int_{0}^{x} t^3\, dt}\right]=- x^3. \end{equation*}
    Differentiating the second term, we first let \(u(x)=2x.\) Then,
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \frac{d}{dx}\left[\displaystyle{ \int_{0}^{2x} t^3\, dt}\right]\amp =\frac{d}{dx}\left[\displaystyle{ \int_{0}^{u(x)} t^3\, dt}\right]\\ \amp =(u(x))^3\frac{du}{dx} \\ \amp =(2x)^3\cdot 2\\ \amp =16x^3. \end{align*}
    Thus,
    🔗
    \begin{align*} F'(x) \amp =\frac{d}{dx}\left[- \displaystyle{ \int_{0}^{x} t^3\, dt}\right]+\frac{d}{dx}\left[\displaystyle{ \int_{0}^{2x} t^3dt}\right]\\ \amp =- x^3+16x^3\\ \amp =15x^3. \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.3.2 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, Part 2: The Evaluation Theorem

    After tireless efforts by mathematicians for approximately 500 years, new techniques emerged that provided scientists with the necessary tools to explain many phenomena. Using calculus, astronomers could finally determine distances in space and map planetary orbits. Everyday financial problems such as calculating marginal costs or predicting total profit could now be handled with simplicity and accuracy. Engineers could calculate the bending strength of materials or the three-dimensional motion of objects. Our view of the world was forever changed with calculus.
    🔗
    The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, Part 2, provides an alternative efficient method to compute definite integrals. It is perhaps the one theorem that you will use apply the most in your continued study of calculus. The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, Part 2 (also known as the Evaluation Theorem) states that if we can find an antiderivative for the integrand, then we can evaluate a definite integral by evaluating the antiderivative at the endpoints of the interval and subtracting. This theorem replaces the need to evaluate a limit of Riemann sums with the task of finding an antiderivative when we wish to evaluate a definite integral exactly.
    🔗

    Theorem 5.3.10 The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, Part 2.

    If \(f\) is continuous on the interval \([a,b]\) and \(F(x)\) is any antiderivative of \(f(x),\) then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \displaystyle{ \int_{a}^{b} f(x)\, dx}=F(b)-F(a). \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Proof.
    Let
    \begin{equation*} a=x_0 \lt x_1 \lt x_2 \lt \dots \lt x_n=b \end{equation*}
    be a regular partition of \([a,b].\) Then, we can write
    🔗
    \begin{align*} F(b)-F(a)\amp =F(x_n)-F(x_0)\\ \amp =[F(x_n)-F(x_{n-1})]+[F(x_{n-1})-F(x_{n-2})]\\ \amp\quad + [F(x_{n-2})-F(x_{n-3})] +\dots +[F(x_1)-F(x_0)]\\ \amp =\displaystyle{\sum_{i=1}^n }[F(x_i)-F(x_{i-1})]. \end{align*}
    Because \(F\) is an antiderivative of \(f\) over \([a,b]\text{,}\) \(F'(x)=f(x)\) for all \(x\) in \([a,b]\text{.}\) For each subinterval \([x_{i-1},x_i]\text{,}\) we can apply the Mean Value Theorem (see The Mean Value Theorem 4.4) to \(F\) to obain a point \(x_i^*\) in \([x_{i-1},x_i]\) such that
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} F(x_i)-F(x_{i-1})=F'(x_i^*)(x_i-x_{i-1})=f(x_i^*)\Delta x. \end{equation*}
    Then, substituting into the previous equation, we have
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} F(b)-F(a)=\displaystyle{\sum_{i=1}^n f(x_i^*)\Delta x}. \end{equation*}
    So the Riemann sums constructed using the evaluation points guaranteed by the Mean Value Theorem are constant and equal to \(F(b)-F(a)\text{.}\) Because \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\text{,}\) \(f\) is integrable on \([a,b]\text{.}\) In particular,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \displaystyle{ \int_{a}^{b} f(x)\, dx}=\lim_{n\to \infty }\displaystyle{ \sum_{i=1}^nf(x_i^*)\Delta x} =F(b)-F(a). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    We often use the notation \(F(x)\biggr\vert_a^b\) or \(F(x)\biggr\vert_{x=a}^{x=b}\) to denote the expression \(F(b)-F(a).\) We use this vertical bar and associated limits \(a\) and \(b\) to indicate that we should evaluate the function \(F(x)\) at the upper limit (in this case, \(b\)), and subtract the value of the function \(F(x)\) evaluated at the lower limit (in this case, \(a\)). This difference is the net change in the function \(F\) from \(x=a\) to \(x=b\text{.}\)
    🔗

    James and Kathy are racing on roller skates. They race along a long, straight track, and whoever has gone the farthest after 5 sec wins a prize.     Solution.
    We need to integrate both functions over the interval \([0,5]\) and see which value is bigger. For James, we want to calculate
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \displaystyle{ \int_{0}^{5} 5+2t \, dt}. \end{equation*}
    Using the power rule, we have
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \displaystyle{ \int_{0}^{5} 5+2t \, dt}\amp =5t+t^2\biggr\vert_0^5\\ \amp =(25+25)-(0+0)=50. \end{align*}

    🔗

    Subsection 5.3.3 The Mean Value Theorem for Integrals

    Now that we have the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, we examine another important theorem, the Mean Value Theorem for Integrals. The Mean Value Theorem for Integrals states that a continuous function on a closed interval takes on its average value at the same point in that interval. The theorem guarantees that if \(f(x)\) is continuous, a point \(c\) exists in an interval \([a,b]\) such that the value of the function at \(c\) is equal to the average value of \(f(x)\) over \([a,b].\) We state this theorem mathematically with the help of the formula for the average value of a function that we presented at the end of the preceding section.
    🔗

    Theorem 5.3.18 The Mean Value Theorem for Integrals.

    If \(f(x)\) is continuous over an interval \([a,b],\) then there is at least one point \(c\) in \([a,b]\) such that
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} f(c)=\frac{1}{b-a} \int_{a}^{b} f(x)\, dx. \end{equation*}
    This formula can also be stated as
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int_{a}^{b} f(x)\, dx=f(c)(b-a). \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Proof.
    Let
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\, dt \end{equation*}
    be the area function for \(f\) on \([a,b]\text{.}\) By the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, Part I, \(A\) is an antiderivative of \(f\) on \([a,b]\text{.}\) Because \(A\) is differentiable on all of \([a,b]\text{,}\) the Mean Value Theorem for Derivatives gives a point \(c\) betwen \(a\) and \(b\) such that
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A'(c)(b-a)=A(b)-A(a). \end{equation*}
    But \(A'(c)=f(c)\) and
    🔗
    \begin{align*} A(b)-A(a) \amp = \int_a^b f(t)\, dt-\int_a^a f(t)\, dt\\ \amp = \int_a^b f(t)\, dt-0\\ \amp = \int_a^b f(x)\, dx \end{align*}
    Putting everything together, we obtain
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int_{a}^{b} f(x)\, dx=f(c)(b-a) \end{equation*}
    or
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} f(c)=\frac{1}{b-a} \int_{a}^{b} f(x)\, dx. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Example 5.3.19 Finding the Average Value of a Function.

    Find the average value of the function \(f(x)=8-2x\) over the interval \([0,4]\) and find \(c\) such that \(f(c)\) equals the average value of the function over \([0,4].\)
